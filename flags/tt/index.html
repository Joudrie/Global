Flag of Trinidad and Tobago

Trinidad and Tobago is a twin-island Caribbean nation just off the northeastern coast of Venezuela and is the birthplace of two distinctive cultural contributions. Trinidad is the origin of the steelpan, the national instrument developed in the early 20th century from oil drums. It is also the birthplace of Caribbean Carnival as it developed, with Trinidad's annual Carnival renowned worldwide for its elaborate masquerade bands and calypso and soca music.

What the flag of Trinidad and Tobago means

Red represents the vitality of the land and its people and the warmth of the sun, white the sea and the purity of the people's hopes, and black their dedication and strength. The flag was adopted at independence from Britain in 1962.

More about the flag

Trinidad and Tobago became independent from Britain on 31 August 1962. Its flag is said to represent the elements of earth, water and fire, and the diagonal black band is read as the bond uniting the two islands. The country is the birthplace of the steelpan and of calypso and soca music.

How to recognise it

Red field with a black diagonal stripe bordered in white from upper left to lower right.

Did you know?

Trinidad is the birthplace of the steel drum (steelpan) — the only acoustic instrument invented in the 20th century.

Flag history of Trinidad and Tobago

Every flag that has flown over Trinidad and Tobago, from today back through time.

1962–present

Trinidad and Tobago (independence)

Trinidad and Tobago's flag features a black stripe edged in white running diagonally from the upper hoist to the lower fly on a red field. The designer, Carlisle Chang, intended red for the fire element and the warmth of the people; black for the dedication and strength that unifies the country; white for the equality of all under the sun. The two islands gained independence together from Britain on August 31, 1962.

1797–1962

British Trinidad & Tobago (colonial era)

Trinidad was a Spanish colony until captured by Britain in 1797; Tobago changed hands between European powers 22 times — more than any other territory in the world — before being ceded to Britain in 1814. The islands were merged into a single crown colony in 1889. Trinidad and Tobago became one of the wealthiest Caribbean nations through oil production, and its multicultural society (African, Indian, European, and Chinese heritage) shaped a unique culture including calypso, soca, and steelband music.

1498–1797

Spanish Trinidad

Columbus reached Trinidad in 1498, and Spain held it for nearly three centuries under the Cross of Burgundy — though it remained a neglected backwater that Spain eventually opened to French Catholic planters, who gave the island much of its Francophone-Creole character before a British fleet took it in 1797.
