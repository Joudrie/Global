Africa

Flag of South Africa

South Africa is the southernmost country in Africa and one of the continent's most economically developed and culturally diverse nations. The country peacefully ended its apartheid system of racial segregation in 1994, holding its first fully democratic elections that year and electing Nelson Mandela as president. South Africa has extraordinary natural diversity spanning the Cape Floristic Region, Kruger National Park, and the Drakensberg Mountains, and is the only country to have voluntarily dismantled its nuclear weapons programme.

What the flag of South Africa means

Adopted for the first fully democratic elections in 1994, the flag was designed by State Herald Frederick Brownell in a matter of weeks. It has no official meaning, but the Y shape is widely seen as different parts of society coming together and moving forward as one. It includes red, white and blue from the Boer republics and Britain, and black, green and gold from the flag of the ANC.

How to recognise it

Unique Y-shaped green chevron splitting red-blue horizontals from a black-yellow-white wedge at the hoist.

Did you know?

South Africa has three capital cities: Pretoria (executive), Cape Town (legislative), and Bloemfontein (judicial).

Flag history of South Africa

Every flag that has flown over South Africa, from today back through time.

1994–present

Rainbow flag

Designed by Frederick Brownell and first flown on 27 April 1994 at the dawn of democracy, it is the only national flag with six colors. The Y-shape is widely read as the convergence of a diverse society onto a single path forward.

1928–1994

Oranje-Blanje-Blou

Based on the orange-white-blue Dutch Prinsenvlag, with three tiny flags centered on the white band — the Union Jack and the flags of the old Orange Free State and Transvaal republics. It became internationally synonymous with the apartheid era.

1910–1928

South African Red Ensign

After the Union of South Africa formed in 1910, the British Red Ensign defaced with the Union coat of arms served as the de facto flag, while the Union Jack remained the only flag with full official status.

1857–1902

South African Republic (Transvaal)

In the interior, Dutch-descended Boer settlers fleeing British rule founded their own republics — the South African Republic (Transvaal), whose green-banded "Vierkleur" flew over the gold-rich Witwatersrand. The discovery of that gold drew the British in, and the two sides fought the bitter Second Boer War (1899–1902); the republic's defeat paved the way for the Union of South Africa.

1854–1902

Orange Free State

The other main Boer republic, the Orange Free State, flew an orange-and-white flag with the Dutch tricolour in the canton. Like the Transvaal it was independent for half a century before being annexed by Britain in the Boer War — both republics' flags were later miniaturised onto the apartheid-era national flag.

1981–1994

Ciskei

A small apartheid-era Bantustan for Xhosa people in the Eastern Cape, dissolved into democratic South Africa in 1994.

1977–1994

Bophuthatswana

A fragmented apartheid-era Bantustan for Tswana people, reabsorbed into South Africa at the end of apartheid.

1976–1994

Transkei

The first of apartheid South Africa's nominally independent Black 'homelands' (Bantustans), recognised by no other country.

1886–1891

Klein Vrystaat

A minuscule Boer 'Little Free State' that voluntarily joined the South African Republic after a few years.

1884–1888

Nieuwe Republiek

A Boer 'New Republic' carved out of Zululand around Vryheid, later joined to the Transvaal.

1882–1883

State of Goshen

A tiny Boer freebooter republic on the Transvaal border, later folded into British Bechuanaland.

1882–1885

Republic of Stellaland

A small Boer republic proclaimed by frontier farmers, named for a comet, annexed into British Bechuanaland.

1870–1880

Griqualand West

A Griqua state on the Cape frontier annexed by Britain after diamonds were found at Kimberley.

1861–1879

Griqualand East

A Griqua state in the eastern Cape settled after a great trek over the Drakensberg, later annexed by the Cape Colony.

1839–1843

Natalia Republic

A short-lived Boer republic in Natal, founded by Voortrekkers after the defeat of the Zulu, soon annexed by Britain.

1835–1864

Republic of Zoutpansberg

An isolated Boer republic in the far north of the Transvaal, eventually absorbed into the South African Republic.

1928–1994

Apartheid South Africa

Based on the Dutch Prinsenvlag, its white stripe carries three miniature flags — the Union Flag, the Orange Free State, and the Transvaal Vierkleur — for the territories of the Union.

1854–1902

Orange Free State

This Boer republic placed the Dutch tricolor in the canton over orange-and-white stripes. The flag was later embedded inside the apartheid-era South African flag.

1857–1902

South African Republic (Transvaal)

The Boer 'Vierkleur' added a green vertical stripe to the Dutch red-white-blue. It flew over Paul Kruger's republic through the Anglo-Boer Wars.

1839–1843

Natalia Republic

A short-lived Voortrekker republic in present-day KwaZulu-Natal, annexed by Britain in 1843; its flag was a Dutch-style design with a white hoist triangle.

1882–1885

Stellaland

A Boer republic — its name means 'star land' for a comet seen at its founding — with a green flag and a single white star, soon annexed into British Bechuanaland.

1882–1883

State of Goshen

A tiny Boer republic near the Transvaal border, which briefly merged with neighbouring Stellaland before Britain absorbed both.

1884–1888

Nieuwe Republiek

A Boer 'New Republic' around Vryheid, carved out of Zulu land as a reward for backing a claimant to the Zulu throne, before joining the Transvaal.

1910–1928

Union of South Africa (1910–1912 ensign)

Before the 1928 'oranje-blanje-blou' was designed, the new Union flew the British Red Ensign defaced with a South African shield — a stopgap that lingered as a de facto national flag for years.

1976–1994

Transkei

The first of the nominally independent apartheid 'homelands', its ochre-white-green flag was recognized by no country but South Africa. Its Xhosa-majority population were stripped of South African citizenship.

1977–1994

Bophuthatswana

A Tswana bantustan scattered across seven non-contiguous enclaves, its orange flag bore a leopard's head. Its casino resort of Sun City became a way for foreigners to sidestep the cultural boycott of South Africa.

1979–1994

Venda

A small Venda-speaking homeland in the far north, one of the four bantustans that accepted nominal 'independence' from apartheid South Africa before all were reabsorbed in 1994.

1981–1994

Ciskei

A Xhosa homeland on the west bank of the Great Kei River; its flag's diagonal stripe and rising sun did little to disguise a state propped up entirely by Pretoria, the scene of the 1992 Bisho massacre.

1981–1994

KwaZulu

The Zulu homeland under Chief Mangosuthu Buthelezi never took the 'independence' offered by apartheid, but flew its own lion-and-shield flag as a self-governing territory until 1994.

1971–1994

Gazankulu

A Tsonga-Shangaan self-governing homeland in the northeast Transvaal; its green flag carried a yellow elephant tusk, and unlike the TBVC states it declined nominal independence.

1972–1994

Lebowa

A self-governing homeland for the Northern Sotho (Pedi) people, its green-and-white flag bearing a stylized head of cattle, before South Africa's homelands were dissolved in 1994.

1974–1994

QwaQwa

The smallest of the homelands, a tiny mountainous enclave for Southern Sotho speakers wedged against Lesotho; its name means 'whiter than white' for the sandstone cliffs above it.
