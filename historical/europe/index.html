Historical flags

Historical flags of Europe

197 kingdoms, empires, republics and short-lived states from Europe that no longer exist, with the flags they flew and the story behind each one.

1867–1918

Austria-Hungary

The civil ensign split 50/50, joining the Austrian red-white-red with Hungary's colors and both shields — the Dual Monarchy never agreed on a single flag for the whole empire.

Today part of: Bosnia and Herzegovina, Hungary, Czech Republic, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia

1918–1941

Kingdom of Yugoslavia

The blue-white-red Pan-Slavic tricolor symbolized the union of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes, flown by the Kingdom until the Axis invasion of 1941.

Today part of: Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Slovenia, North Macedonia, Montenegro, Kosovo

1945–1992

Socialist Yugoslavia

Tito's Partisans added a red, gold-bordered star to the tricolor; it marked the country's communist character until the federation's violent breakup in the 1990s.

Today part of: Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Slovenia, North Macedonia, Montenegro, Kosovo

1920–1992

Czechoslovakia

Adopted in 1920 with a blue hoist wedge to tell it apart from Poland's flag. Czechia kept it after the 1993 split, despite a clause meant to bar successor states from reusing it.

Today part of: Czech Republic, Slovakia

1871–1918

German Empire

The black-white-red tricolor combined Prussia's black-and-white with the Hanseatic red-and-white. It was officially adopted as the national flag only in 1892.

Today part of: Germany

1919–1933

Weimar Republic

The black-red-gold tricolor revived the colors of the 1848 liberal revolution, deliberately tying the new democracy to earlier anti-autocratic movements.

Today part of: Germany

1935–1945

Nazi Germany

The swastika flag of the Nazi regime, which started the Second World War in Europe and carried out the Holocaust, became Germany's sole national flag in 1935. It was banned after the regime's defeat in 1945; its public display is banned in Germany outside education, art and research, and the country returned to the black-red-gold of the Weimar era.

Today part of: Germany

1959–1990

East Germany (GDR)

The hammer-and-compass-in-wheat emblem represented workers, intelligentsia, and farmers. Before 1959 East and West Germany flew the identical plain tricolor.

Today part of: Germany

1701–1918

Kingdom of Prussia

The black eagle on a white field derives from the Teutonic Knights. Prussia's black-and-white later supplied two of the three colors of the German Empire's flag.

Today part of: Germany

1858–1896

Russian Empire

The black-yellow-white 'imperial colors' were drawn from the state coat of arms by Alexander II, before Nicholas II demoted them for the white-blue-red tricolor.

Today part of: Finland, Ukraine, Belarus

1922–1991

Soviet Union

The gold hammer and sickle beneath a red star stood for the worker-peasant alliance. The exact shade and proportions were fixed by Soviet law in 1955.

Today part of: Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia

1261–1453

Byzantine Empire

The four gold firesteels (often glossed 'King of Kings, Ruling over Kings') were the emblem of the Palaiologos dynasty, the last imperial house, which fell with Constantinople in 1453.

Today part of: Greece, Turkey

962–1806

Holy Roman Empire

The black double-headed eagle on gold was the Emperor's banner. Its two heads symbolized claimed authority over both the secular and the spiritual realms.

Today part of: Germany

697–1797

Republic of Venice

The golden winged Lion of St Mark holds an open Gospel; by tradition the book was shown open in peacetime and the lion grasped a sword in war.

Today part of: Italy

1816–1861

Kingdom of the Two Sicilies

The white Bourbon field carries an elaborate coat of arms of the many southern Italian domains the dynasty ruled, until Garibaldi's forces conquered it in 1860–61.

Today part of: Italy

1825–1870

Papal States

The yellow-and-white vertical bicolor with the crossed keys of St Peter beneath the papal tiara is the direct ancestor of today's Vatican City flag.

Today part of: Vatican City

1816–1848

Kingdom of Sardinia

A white field with the red Savoy cross in a blue border. After 1848 Sardinia switched to the tricolor with the Savoy shield, which became the basis of the Kingdom of Italy's flag.

Today part of: Italy

1099–1797

Republic of Genoa

The red cross of St George on white was Genoa's medieval ensign. By one account England paid Genoa in 1190 for the right to fly it for naval protection.

Today part of: Italy

1920–1939

Free City of Danzig

Two white crosses and a gold crown on red, from the city's medieval arms. This League of Nations-administered free city was the flashpoint where World War II began.

Today part of: Poland

1797–1802

Cisalpine Republic

This Napoleonic sister republic in northern Italy flew a vertical green-white-red tricolor modeled on revolutionary France — a direct forerunner of the modern Italian flag.

Today part of: Italy

1115–1532

Republic of Florence

The red giglio — a stylized lily — on white was the emblem of the medieval commune. Florence reversed the earlier white-on-red Ghibelline lily to mark its Guelph allegiance.

Today part of: Italy

1125–1555

Republic of Siena

The black-and-white Balzana is said to recall the white and black smoke that rose when Siena's legendary founders Senius and Aschius made sacrifices on the city's hills.

Today part of: Italy

1192–1797

Republic of Noli

One of the smallest and least-known Italian maritime republics, this tiny Ligurian town governed itself for six centuries under the protection of allied Genoa.

Today part of: Italy

1416–1860

Duchy of Savoy

The white cross on red of the House of Savoy, the dynasty that ruled an Alpine state straddling France and Italy and eventually became the royal house of a united Italy.

Today part of: Italy

1452–1859

Duchy of Modena and Reggio

Ruled by the Este dynasty and later the Austrian Habsburg-Este line, the duchy flew a tricolour charged with the Este eagle until it was absorbed into unified Italy.

Today part of: Italy

1530–1708

Duchy of Mantua

The Gonzaga dynasty's duchy in the Po plain, a great Renaissance art and music centre, flew a flag quartered with their eagles and lions before passing to Austria.

Today part of: Italy

1471–1598

Duchy of Ferrara

The Este capital and a brilliant Renaissance court; when the last legitimate Este duke died without heirs in 1597, the Pope reclaimed Ferrara for the Papal States.

Today part of: Italy

1443–1631

Duchy of Urbino

The Montefeltro and later Della Rovere dukes made Urbino one of the foremost humanist courts of the Renaissance, birthplace of the painter Raphael.

Today part of: Italy

961–1574

Marquisate of Montferrat

A long-lived northern Italian march whose Aleramici marquises led crusades and even briefly held the throne of the Kingdom of Thessalonica.

Today part of: Italy

1442–1816

Kingdom of Naples

The independent southern kingdom before its 1816 merger with Sicily; its white flag bore the arms of the successive Aragonese, Spanish, and Bourbon dynasties that ruled it.

Today part of: Italy

1808–1815

Kingdom of Naples (Napoleonic)

Under Napoleon's marshal Joachim Murat, Naples flew a white field with a red border and a central emblem, before Murat was deposed and executed in 1815.

Today part of: Italy

1796–1797

Transpadane Republic

A short-lived Napoleonic sister republic around Milan that flew a vertical green-white-red tricolour, soon merged with the Cispadane Republic to form the Cisalpine Republic.

Today part of: Italy

1798–1799

Roman Republic (1798)

A Napoleonic republic proclaimed after French troops seized Rome and deposed Pope Pius VI; its black-white-red tricolour flew until Neapolitan and Austrian forces restored papal rule.

Today part of: Italy

1919–1920

Italian Regency of Carnaro

Poet Gabriele D'Annunzio's quasi-anarchic statelet at Fiume, whose flag bore the constellation of Ursa Major and the motto 'Quis contra nos?' — 'Who is against us?'.

Today part of: Croatia

1944

Partisan Republic of Ossola

A free zone liberated by anti-Fascist partisans in the Alps near the Swiss border, self-governing for forty days in 1944 before German and Fascist forces retook it.

Today part of: Italy

1944

Partisan Republic of Alba

A Piedmontese town held free by partisans for 23 days in 1944, immortalized in Beppe Fenoglio's novel 'I ventitré giorni della città di Alba'.

Today part of: Italy

1943–1945

Italian Social Republic

Mussolini's German-backed rump state in northern Italy, often called the Republic of Salò; its war flag added a fasces-clutching eagle to the tricolour.

Today part of: Italy

1113–present

Sovereign Military Order of Malta

The white eight-pointed Maltese cross on red is the Knights Hospitaller's 'works' flag; the eight points are traditionally said to represent the eight Beatitudes.

Today part of: Malta

1822–1978

Kingdom of Greece

The plain blue field with a white cross was the official land flag from 1822. The blue-and-white striped version initially served only as the naval ensign.

Today part of: Greece

1506–1785

Cross of Burgundy

The red saw-tooth saltire represents two crossed, roughly-pruned branches. It flew over Spanish armies and colonies across the Americas for nearly three centuries.

Today part of: Spain

1931–1939

Second Spanish Republic

The republican tricolor added a purple lower band popularly linked to Castile, replacing the red-yellow monarchist flag until Franco's victory in 1939.

Today part of: Spain

1861–1946

Kingdom of Italy

The green-white-red tricolor charged with the crowned shield of the House of Savoy, flown through both World Wars until the 1946 referendum abolished the monarchy.

Today part of: Italy

987–1792

Kingdom of France

The white field strewn with golden fleurs-de-lis was the banner of the Bourbon monarchy. White, not a tricolor, symbolized the king before the Revolution.

Today part of: France

1765–1860

Grand Duchy of Tuscany

The red-white-red triband charged with the grand-ducal crown was used by the Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes who ruled Florence and Tuscany.

Today part of: Italy

1855–1891

Grand Duchy of Baden

The red-over-yellow bicolor was Baden's flag from 1855. The southwestern grand duchy was one of the states that unified into the German Empire in 1871.

Today part of: Germany

1837–1866

Kingdom of Hanover

King Ernst August introduced the yellow-over-white bicolor in 1837 when Hanover's personal union with Britain ended. Its emblem was the white Saxon horse.

Today part of: Germany

1806–1918

Kingdom of Bavaria

The white-and-blue lozenge 'Rautenflagge' derives from the medieval arms of the Wittelsbachs. By rule it must show at least 21 lozenges with a white one in the top-right.

Today part of: Germany

1807–1813

Kingdom of Westphalia

This Napoleonic kingdom, ruled by Jérôme Bonaparte, flew a white-and-blue bicolor with crowned arms combining a Napoleonic eagle with the silver Saxon horse.

Today part of: Germany

1918–1920

Kuban People's Republic

An anti-Bolshevik Cossack state on the Kuban river; its blue-raspberry-green bands stood for Cossacks, non-Cossack residents, and highlanders.

Today part of: Russia

1918–1920

Don Republic

The Don Cossack state's blue-yellow-red tricolor stood for the Don Cossacks, Kalmyks, and Russian non-Cossack residents during the Russian Civil War.

Today part of: Russia

1917–1921

Mountainous Republic of the Northern Caucasus

Green and white stripes with a blue canton bearing seven stars for the seven main highland peoples of the North Caucasus.

Today part of: Russia

1920–1922

Republic of Central Lithuania

A Polish client state around Vilnius; its red field bore the Polish White Eagle and the Lithuanian Vytis knight side by side, before annexation by Poland in 1922.

Today part of: Lithuania

1918–1920

Lemko-Rusyn Republic

A pro-Russian Rusyn republic centered on Florynka in the western Lemko region, flying a blue-yellow-green tricolor after the collapse of Austria-Hungary.

Today part of: Poland

medieval–present

City of Gouda

Red-and-white vertical stripes with three silver six-pointed stars — the civic banner of the Dutch cheese city of Gouda, of medieval origin.

Today part of: Netherlands

1848–1849

Republic of San Marco

Daniele Manin's revolutionary Venetian republic flew the Italian tricolor with the winged Lion of St Mark in the canton, until Austria retook Venice in 1849.

Today part of: Italy

1440–1826

Republic of Cospaia

A tiny accidental republic on the Tuscan–Papal border, created by a surveying error and famous for freely growing tobacco. Its flag is divided black and white diagonally.

Today part of: Italy

medieval–1868

Couto Misto

A self-governing 'mixed county' of three villages on the Galicia–Portugal border with its own asylum rights, partitioned between Spain and Portugal in 1864.

Today part of: Spain

1816–1920

Neutral Moresnet

A neutral zinc-mining territory jointly administered by Prussia and the Netherlands (later Belgium) after the Congress of Vienna. Its flag is a black-white-blue tricolor.

Today part of: Belgium

1920–1924

Free State of Fiume

A short-lived independent city-state — modern Rijeka, Croatia — created by the Treaty of Rapallo and annexed by Italy in 1924. Its flag is a burgundy-yellow-blue tricolor.

Today part of: Croatia

1898–1913

Cretan State

Autonomous Crete under Prince George of Greece; its blue flag with a white cross adds a red canton with a white star, symbolizing remaining Ottoman suzerainty.

Today part of: Greece

1914

Principality of Albania (1914)

The brief principality under Prince Wilhelm of Wied: a deep-red field with a large black double-headed eagle, established just after Albanian independence.

Today part of: Albania

1916

Irish Republic (1916)

The green flag with gold lettering 'Irish Republic' flown over Dublin's General Post Office during the Easter Rising. The original captured banner survives in Dublin.

Today part of: Ireland

1795–1806

Batavian Republic

The Dutch revolutionary republic's flag placed a white canton on the tricolor enclosing a seated liberty maiden with a liberty-cap spear and the Dutch lion.

Today part of: Netherlands

1919–1920

North Caucasian Emirate

A short-lived theocratic emirate under Uzun Hajji spanning Chechnya and Dagestan during the Russian Civil War; its green flag bears three white stars above a crescent.

Today part of: Russia

1918–1921

Free Territory (Makhnovshchina)

The Ukrainian anarchist movement under Nestor Makhno flew black banners reading 'Liberty or Death'. The popular skull-and-crossbones version is a debunked Soviet-propaganda myth.

Today part of: Ukraine

1921–1926

Republic of the Rif

Abd el-Krim's Berber state during the Rif War: a red field with a white diamond holding a green crescent and a six-pointed star.

Today part of: Morocco

1736

Kingdom of Corsica (1736)

The white Moor's-head flag of the brief kingdom created when German adventurer Theodore von Neuhoff was crowned King of Corsica for a few months in 1736.

Today part of: France

1794–1796

Anglo-Corsican Kingdom

Combined the British Union Jack with the Corsican Moor's head during the brief union under George III in the French Revolutionary Wars.

Today part of: France

1642–1652

Confederate Ireland

The Catholic Confederation based at Kilkenny flew a green field with a gold harp and a white canton bearing a red cross during the Eleven Years' War.

Today part of: Ireland

1800–1807

Septinsular Republic

A blue field with the golden Lion of St Mark holding seven arrows for the seven Ionian islands — the first autonomous Greek state of the modern era.

Today part of: Greece

1941–1944

Principality of Pindus

A green-white-blue flag tied to a WWII Axis-sponsored Aromanian (Vlach) state in the Pindus mountains, whose actual existence is disputed by historians.

Today part of: Greece

1797–1798

Anconine Republic

A Napoleonic sister republic around the Adriatic port of Ancona, flying a light blue–yellow–red tricolor with the republic's name lettered across it.

Today part of: Italy

1799

Parthenopean Republic

A short-lived Neapolitan sister republic that joined French blue with the yellow and red of Naples, crushed within months by a royalist peasant army.

Today part of: Italy

1802–1810

Rhodanic Republic

The Napoleonic-era republic of Valais flew a vertical red-and-white field with stars for its districts, before being absorbed directly into France.

Today part of: Switzerland

1796–1797

Cispadane Republic

The first Italian horizontal red-white-green tricolor, with a central quiver of four arrows. Adopted on 7 January 1797, it is commemorated as Italy's Tricolour Day.

Today part of: Italy

1358–1808

Republic of Ragusa

The Adriatic merchant republic of Dubrovnik flew a white flag bearing St Blaise, its patron, holding a model of the walled city. Its motto was 'Libertas'.

Today part of: Croatia

1388–1860

County of Nice

A red eagle perched on three green hills on white — the arms of Nice during its centuries under the House of Savoy, before France annexed it in 1860.

Today part of: France

1180–1918

Duchy of Styria

The white-over-green Landesfarben of the Habsburg duchy; its arms bear a white fire-breathing panther. Styria is now split between Austria and Slovenia.

Today part of: Austria

976–1918

Duchy of Carinthia

The yellow-red-white crown-land flag of one of the oldest duchies of the Holy Roman Empire, whose arms show three black lions on gold.

Today part of: Austria

1500–1918

County of Gorizia (Görz)

A white-and-red bicolour for the Habsburg princely county on the Adriatic frontier, whose arms feature a gold lion. Its land is now split between Italy and Slovenia.

Today part of: Slovenia

1171–1541

Lordship of Ireland

Three gold crowns on light blue with a white border, the banner of the medieval English lordship before the gold-harp arms replaced it under Henry VIII.

Today part of: Ireland

1079–1265

Kingdom of Mann

The gold three-legged triskelion on red descends from the Norse-Gaelic Kingdom of Mann and the Isles, which once ruled the Irish Sea from the Isle of Man.

Today part of: United Kingdom

1557–1867

Principality of Mingrelia

A white flag with a St George crest, flown by the Dadiani princes of western Georgia until the principality was absorbed by the Russian Empire.

Today part of: Georgia

1834–1912

Principality of Samos

A cross flag with two red and two blue quarters, the red marking Ottoman suzerainty over this autonomous Greek island principality, which united with Greece in 1912.

Today part of: Greece

1339–1881

Monaco (Grimaldi lozenge banner)

The Grimaldi armorial banner of red-and-white lozenges, the dynasty's historic flag before Monaco adopted its plain red-over-white bicolour in 1881.

Today part of: Monaco

1836–1934

Kingdom of Tavolara

A tiny island kingdom off Sardinia ruled by the Bertoleoni family, sometimes called the smallest kingdom in the world. Its flag bears a gold star beneath a crown.

Today part of: Italy

1561–1795

Duchy of Courland and Semigallia

A Polish-Lithuanian vassal duchy in modern Latvia that — astonishingly — founded colonies on Tobago in the Caribbean and an island in the Gambia in the 1650s.

Today part of: Latvia

1649–1764

Cossack Hetmanate

The Cossack state of central Ukraine, founded by Bohdan Khmelnytsky's uprising, flew a crimson banner with a Cossack bearing a musket.

Today part of: Ukraine

1920–1922

Far Eastern Republic

A nominally independent buffer state the Bolsheviks set up across Siberia to avoid war with Japan; once the Japanese withdrew, it was absorbed into Soviet Russia.

Today part of: Russia

1918

Donets–Krivoy Rog Soviet Republic

A short-lived Bolshevik republic in the Donbas industrial basin that tried to stay part of Soviet Russia rather than join Ukraine — dissolved within months.

Today part of: Ukraine

1921–1931

SSR of Abkhazia

For a decade Abkhazia was a full Soviet republic with a hammer-and-sickle flag, before Stalin downgraded it to an autonomous republic within Georgia.

Today part of: Georgia

1918

Kingdom of Finland

After independence Finland briefly elected a German prince as king and designed lion-bearing royal flags — but Germany's defeat in WWI ended the monarchy before he ever arrived.

Today part of: Finland

1814–1815

Principality of Elba

The tiny Mediterranean island Napoleon was given to rule during his first exile; he designed its white flag with a red bar and three gold bees before escaping to retake France.

Today part of: Italy

1801–1807

Kingdom of Etruria

A short-lived Napoleonic client kingdom in Tuscany under the Bourbon-Parma dynasty, before Napoleon annexed it directly into France.

Today part of: Italy

1207–1579

Duchy of the Archipelago

A Venetian crusader state ruling the Cyclades from Naxos, under the Sanudo dynasty whose white banner bore a red cross and four black crosslets.

Today part of: Greece

1816–1849

Kingdom of Illyria

An Austrian crown land carved from former Napoleonic territory along the Adriatic, covering parts of modern Slovenia, Croatia, and Austria.

Today part of: Slovenia

1913–1914

Republic of Central Albania

Warlord Essad Pasha Toptani's short-lived statelet around Durrës during Albania's chaotic birth, flying a red flag with a white star.

Today part of: Albania

1917–1922

Republic of Pontus

A proposed homeland for the Pontic Greeks of the Black Sea coast, never realized; its black flag bore the eagle of the medieval Empire of Trebizond.

Today part of: Greece

1917–1918

Crimean People's Republic

One of the first secular Muslim-led republics in the world, flying the sky-blue Kök Bayraq with a golden Tatar tamga, before the Bolsheviks crushed it within months.

Today part of: Ukraine

1545–1859

Duchy of Parma

A northern Italian duchy long ruled by the Farnese and later Bourbon-Parma dynasties, its yellow flag bearing a blue fleur-de-lis shield, absorbed into unified Italy.

Today part of: Italy

1805–1809

Principality of Lucca and Piombino

A Napoleonic state given to the emperor's sister Elisa Bonaparte, the only one of his siblings to rule in her own right.

Today part of: Italy

1806–1918

Kingdom of Saxony

The white-over-green bicolor of the Wettin kings, who ruled from Dresden and joined the German Empire in 1871 while keeping their own monarch until 1918.

Today part of: Germany

1806–1918

Kingdom of Württemberg

Napoleon elevated the duchy of Württemberg to a kingdom in 1806; its black-over-red bicolor flew over Stuttgart until the monarchy fell at the end of World War I.

Today part of: Germany

1806–1918

Grand Duchy of Hesse

A red-over-white bicolor for the Hesse-Darmstadt grand dukes, whose dynasty married into Europe's thrones — the last Tsarina of Russia was a Hessian princess.

Today part of: Germany

1803–1866

Electorate of Hesse

The Hesse-Kassel state, the only German principality to keep the title 'Elector' after the Holy Roman Empire dissolved, until Prussia annexed it in 1866.

Today part of: Germany

1815–1918

Grand Duchies of Mecklenburg

The blue-yellow-red tricolor shared by the two Mecklenburg grand duchies (Schwerin and Strelitz) on the Baltic coast, ruled by branches of the same Obotrite dynasty.

Today part of: Germany

1815–1918

Grand Duchy of Oldenburg

A blue-red bicolor for the northwest German grand duchy whose ruling house also briefly held the thrones of Denmark, Greece, and Russia through dynastic marriage.

Today part of: Germany

1815–1918

Duchy of Brunswick

The blue-over-yellow bicolor of the Welf dukes; its 'Black Duke' Frederick William raised a famous corps of black-clad hussars to fight Napoleon and died at Quatre Bras.

Today part of: Germany

1806–1866

Duchy of Nassau

An orange-blue bicolor for the duchy on the Rhine and Lahn; its ruling house gave the Netherlands its royal dynasty, the House of Orange-Nassau.

Today part of: Germany

1809–1918

Grand Duchy of Saxe-Weimar-Eisenach

The black-yellow-green flag of the Thuringian grand duchy whose Weimar court, under Goethe and Schiller, became the cultural heart of German classicism.

Today part of: Germany

1826–1918

Duchy of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha

A green-white bicolor for the small Thuringian duchy that became the 'stud farm of Europe' — its princes married into the British, Belgian, Portuguese, and Bulgarian thrones.

Today part of: Germany

1778–1918

Principalities of Reuss

The tiny Thuringian principalities famous for naming every male ruler Heinrich and numbering them in sequence — the elder line ran the count up to Heinrich LXVII.

Today part of: Germany

1816–1918

Principalities of Schwarzburg

A blue-white bicolor for the two small Thuringian principalities of Rudolstadt and Sondershausen, ruled by one of Germany's oldest comital dynasties.

Today part of: Germany

1815–1918

Principality of Lippe

A yellow-red bicolor for the small principality around Detmold, near the Teutoburg Forest where Germanic tribes annihilated three Roman legions in AD 9.

Today part of: Germany

1807–1918

Principality of Schaumburg-Lippe

One of the smallest German states, a blue-red-white tricolor principality of barely 50,000 people that nonetheless survived as a sovereign state until 1918.

Today part of: Germany

1863–1918

Duchy of Anhalt

A green-white bicolor for the central German duchy, ancestral home of the Ascanian dynasty and birthplace of Catherine the Great of Russia.

Today part of: Germany

1819–1919

Free City of Hamburg

The white castle on red is the Hanseatic city-state's medieval emblem; its three towers and the cross-topped gate symbolize the free port's civic independence.

Today part of: Germany

1813–1919

Free City of Bremen

The red-and-white 'Speckflagge' (bacon flag) of checkered stripes flew over the Hanseatic port-republic, one of the founding member states of the German Empire.

Today part of: Germany

1226–1937

Free City of Lübeck

The white-over-red bicolor of the 'Queen of the Hanseatic League', the city that led the medieval Baltic trade network before the Nazis stripped its statehood in 1937.

Today part of: Germany

1815–1866

Free City of Frankfurt

The white-over-red city republic that hosted the German Confederation's parliament, until Prussia annexed it for siding against it in the 1866 war.

Today part of: Germany

1815–1866

German Confederation

The loose league of German states formed after Napoleon's defeat flew a black-red-gold war flag — the same liberal colors later adopted by unified Germany.

Today part of: Germany

1947–1956

Saar Protectorate

A French-administered, semi-autonomous state after WWII, its flag a Nordic cross in French blue and red. A 1955 referendum returned the coal-rich Saar to West Germany.

Today part of: Germany

1806–1813

Grand Duchy of Berg

A Napoleonic client state on the Rhine, first ruled by Marshal Murat; its white flag bore a red lion before it was dissolved after Napoleon's defeat.

Today part of: Germany

1032–1477

Duchy of Burgundy

The Valois dukes of Burgundy built one of the richest states in late-medieval Europe, rivaling the French crown, until Duke Charles the Bold fell at the Battle of Nancy in 1477.

Today part of: France

939–1547

Duchy of Brittany

The independent Breton duchy on France's Atlantic peninsula; its modern black-and-white 'Gwenn ha du' flag recalls the duchy absorbed into France through royal marriage in 1532.

Today part of: France

959–1766

Duchy of Lorraine

A gold flag with a red diagonal band and three white eaglets; the duchy on the German frontier passed to France only after its last duke became Holy Roman Emperor.

Today part of: France

1274–1791

Comtat Venaissin

A papal enclave around Avignon ruled directly by the Popes for over five centuries — even surrounding the Avignon papacy — until France annexed it during the Revolution.

Today part of: France

972–1481

County of Provence

The gold-and-red striped banner of the medieval county, ruled by the House of Barcelona and then Anjou, before it was bequeathed to the French crown in 1481.

Today part of: France

1011–1607

County of Foix

A Pyrenean county whose arms joined the gold-and-red pales of Foix with the cows of Béarn; its counts became kings of Navarre, and one, Henry, became King of France.

Today part of: France

910–1833

Kingdom of Galicia

One of medieval Europe's oldest kingdoms, in Spain's far northwest; its modern white flag with a blue diagonal recalls the realm home to the pilgrim shrine of Santiago de Compostela.

Today part of: Spain

824–1620

Kingdom of Navarre

The Basque Pyrenean kingdom whose red flag bears golden chains, said to commemorate Navarrese knights breaking the chains around the Almohad caliph's tent in 1212.

Today part of: Spain

1162–1716

Crown of Aragon

The four red bars on gold — the Senyera — flew over a Mediterranean empire that ruled Catalonia, Valencia, the Balearics, Sardinia, Sicily, and at times Naples and Athens.

Today part of: Spain

1162–1714

Principality of Catalonia

The Senyera of four red bars on gold flew over the Catalan lands within the Crown of Aragon; Barcelona's fall in 1714 ended its self-government, an event still marked each September 11.

Today part of: Spain

910–1230

Kingdom of León

The purple lion on white of the early medieval Christian kingdom that drove the Reconquista; its parliament of 1188 is often called the oldest in Europe.

Today part of: Spain

718–924

Kingdom of Asturias

The blue flag with the gold Victory Cross marks the first Christian kingdom of the Reconquista, founded by Pelagius after his victory at Covadonga against the Moors.

Today part of: Spain

1231–1715

Kingdom of Majorca

A short-lived independent Balearic kingdom carved out by James I of Aragon for a younger son, ruling the islands plus Roussillon and Montpellier on the mainland.

Today part of: Spain

1238–1707

Kingdom of Valencia

A realm of the Crown of Aragon whose Senyera added a crowned blue stripe at the hoist; it was abolished by the Bourbons after backing the losing side in the War of Spanish Succession.

Today part of: Spain

1139–1910

Kingdom of Portugal

The white-and-blue royal banner with the shield of five 'quinas' flew over the seafaring kingdom that launched the Age of Discovery, until the 1910 republican revolution.

Today part of: Portugal

1580–1640

Iberian Union

For sixty years the Spanish and Portuguese crowns were joined under one Habsburg king, briefly creating a single empire spanning the globe, until Portugal revolted in 1640.

Today part of: Spain, Portugal

1807–1815

Duchy of Warsaw

Napoleon's Polish client state, a brief restoration of statehood after the Partitions; its white-and-crimson flag preceded the modern Polish flag by a century.

Today part of: Poland

1569–1795

Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

One of the largest states in Europe, an elective monarchy with an early constitution and a 'noble democracy', partitioned out of existence by Russia, Prussia, and Austria.

Today part of: Poland, Lithuania

1815–1846

Free City of Kraków

A tiny independent city-republic created at the Congress of Vienna, the last fragment of sovereign Poland, until Austria annexed it after a failed 1846 uprising.

Today part of: Poland

1947–1989

Polish People's Republic

The Soviet-aligned communist state flew the same white-and-red bicolor as the republic before and after it, the eagle on its arms shorn of its royal crown until 1989.

Today part of: Poland

1949–1991

Ukrainian SSR

A red flag with a lower azure band and a gold hammer, sickle and star, flown by Soviet Ukraine — which, unusually, held its own seat at the United Nations.

Today part of: Ukraine

1951–1991

Byelorussian SSR

Red over a green band with a white folk-ornament stripe at the hoist; modern Belarus readopted a near-identical design in 1995, dropping only the hammer and sickle.

Today part of: Belarus

1953–1990

Lithuanian SSR

A red flag with white and green lower bands, flown over Soviet Lithuania until the republic became the first to declare independence from the USSR in 1990.

Today part of: Lithuania

1953–1990

Latvian SSR

A red flag whose lower edge bore stylized white waves of the Baltic Sea beneath the hammer, sickle and star, used until Latvia restored its independence.

Today part of: Latvia

1953–1990

Estonian SSR

A red flag with a wavy blue-and-white Baltic band at the base, the Soviet flag of Estonia, replaced when the blue-black-white national tricolor was restored in 1990.

Today part of: Estonia

1952–1990

Moldavian SSR

A red flag with a central green stripe and the hammer, sickle and star, flown by Soviet Moldova before the country adopted a Romanian-style tricolor in 1990.

Today part of: Moldova

1954–1991

Russian SFSR

The largest Soviet republic flew a red flag with a thin blue stripe down the hoist; the RSFSR was the core of the USSR and became the Russian Federation in 1991.

Today part of: Russia

1438–1552

Kazan Khanate

A Tatar successor state of the Golden Horde on the Volga, conquered by Ivan the Terrible in 1552 — a turning point in Russia's eastward expansion.

Today part of: Russia

1815–1864

United States of the Ionian Islands

A British protectorate over the Ionian Islands, flying the Lion of St Mark on a Union-Jack-cantoned flag, until the islands were ceded to Greece in 1864.

Today part of: Greece

1918–1919

Belarusian People's Republic

The white-red-white flag of the short-lived republic declared during WWI; suppressed by the Bolsheviks, it returned as independent Belarus's flag from 1991 to 1995.

Today part of: Belarus

1236–1795

Grand Duchy of Lithuania

The red banner with the white Vytis — an armored knight on a charging horse — flew over a realm that at its height stretched from the Baltic to the Black Sea.

Today part of: Lithuania

1441–1783

Crimean Khanate

A blue flag with the golden Giray-dynasty tamga; the last remnant of the Golden Horde, it dominated the northern Black Sea steppe until Catherine the Great annexed it.

Today part of: Ukraine

1490–1598

Khanate of Sibir

The Turco-Mongol khanate beyond the Urals that gave Siberia its name; its conquest by the Cossack Yermak opened Russia's vast eastward expansion across Asia.

Today part of: Russia

1438–1552

Khanate of Kazan

A Tatar successor state of the Golden Horde on the Volga; Ivan the Terrible's bloody siege and capture of Kazan in 1552 is commemorated by Moscow's St Basil's Cathedral.

Today part of: Russia

1466–1556

Khanate of Astrakhan

A small Tatar khanate guarding the Volga delta and the Caspian trade, the last of the Golden Horde's successors, annexed by Ivan the Terrible four years after Kazan.

Today part of: Russia

1242–1502

Golden Horde

The Mongol khanate that held the Russian principalities under the 'Tatar Yoke' for over two centuries; this white banner with a crescent appears on a 1339 Genoese map.

Today part of: Russia

1918

Idel-Ural State

A short-lived Tatar-Bashkir republic on the Volga proclaimed amid the Russian Revolution, crushed by the Bolsheviks within weeks of its declaration.

Today part of: Russia

1918–1922

Green Ukraine

An attempt by Ukrainian settlers in the Russian Far East to form an autonomous state around the Amur and Pacific coast, suppressed by the Bolsheviks.

Today part of: Russia

1919

Hungarian Soviet Republic

Béla Kun's plain red communist state, the second Soviet republic in the world after Russia, which lasted just 133 days before Romanian troops toppled it.

Today part of: Hungary

1949–1989

Hungarian People's Republic

The communist 'Rákosi coat of arms' on the tricolor was so hated that 1956 revolutionaries cut it out, leaving a hole-in-the-flag that became the uprising's emblem.

Today part of: Hungary

1570–1711

Principality of Transylvania

A largely autonomous Ottoman vassal principality and Protestant stronghold; its blue-and-gold flag joined the symbols of its Hungarian, Székely, and Saxon 'nations'.

Today part of: Romania

1198–1918

Kingdom of Bohemia

The white-over-red bicolor of the Czech lands ruled from Prague; its kings were prince-electors of the Holy Roman Empire, and one, Charles IV, became Emperor himself.

Today part of: Czech Republic

1182–1918

Margraviate of Moravia

The eastern Czech land, a margraviate within the Bohemian crown; its yellow-and-red checkered eagle gives the modern Moravian flag its distinctive look.

Today part of: Czech Republic

1939–1945

First Slovak Republic

A clerical-nationalist client state of Nazi Germany under Jozef Tiso, the first time Slovakia existed as a separate state; it flew a plain white-blue-red tricolor.

Today part of: Slovakia

Kingdom of Serbia

The red-blue-white tricolor with the royal arms flew over the independent Serbian kingdom whose alliances and crisis with Austria-Hungary helped ignite World War I.

Today part of: Serbia

Principality of Serbia

An autonomous principality that won self-rule from the Ottomans under the rival Obrenović and Karađorđević dynasties before being raised to a kingdom in 1882.

Today part of: Serbia

Kingdom of Montenegro

The red flag bearing the crowned cypher of King Nikola I, ruler of the small Balkan mountain kingdom that merged into Yugoslavia after World War I.

Today part of: Montenegro

Kingdom of Bulgaria

The white-green-red tricolor of the 'Third Bulgarian State', a kingdom under the Saxe-Coburg-Gotha tsars, abolished by referendum in favor of a communist republic in 1946.

Kingdom of Romania

The blue-yellow-red tricolor of the Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen kings, who doubled the country's size after World War I before the communists forced King Michael to abdicate.

Principality of Wallachia

A Danubian principality long under Ottoman suzerainty, ruled by voivodes including Vlad the Impaler; it united with Moldavia in 1859 to form modern Romania.

Principality of Moldavia

The eastern Romanian principality of Stephen the Great, who held off the Ottomans for decades; its union with Wallachia created the United Principalities of Romania.

The last independent medieval Serbian state, a despotate that briefly flourished at Smederevo before the Ottomans conquered it, ending Serbian statehood for centuries.

A blue-and-yellow flag for the Habsburg crown land along the Adriatic coast, governed from Zadar, whose territory is now part of Croatia.

An autonomous Ottoman province created by the Congress of Berlin, with a white flag and green canton; it unilaterally united with Bulgaria after a bloodless 1885 coup.

The Axis puppet state run by the fascist Ustaše, responsible for atrocities against Serbs, Jews, and Roma; its flag added the Ustaše's 'U' badge to the Croatian checkerboard.

First Republic of Armenia

The brief independent republic born from the collapse of the Russian and Ottoman empires; its red-blue-orange tricolor was readopted when Armenia regained independence in 1991.

The first secular democratic republic in the Muslim world, which gave women the vote before many Western states; its blue-red-green flag flies over independent Azerbaijan again today.

A red flag with a sunburst-and-hammer roundel in a blue canton; Soviet Georgia was the birthplace of Joseph Stalin, in the town of Gori.

A red flag with a horizontal blue stripe and the hammer, sickle and star, flown over Soviet Armenia until the national tricolor was restored in 1990.

Democratic Republic of Georgia

A Menshevik-led social-democratic republic with a wine-red flag, conquered by the Red Army in 1921; its flag flew again over independent Georgia from 1990 to 2004.

The medieval Christian kingdom that reached its 'Golden Age' under Queen Tamar, ruling much of the Caucasus before Mongol invasions fractured it into rival realms.

The dynastic union that joined Denmark, Sweden, and Norway under a single monarch for over a century, until Gustav Vasa led Sweden to break away in 1523.

A red-and-yellow Nordic-cross flag for the southern provinces that were Danish for centuries before Sweden conquered them in 1658 — a frontier fought over for generations.

The white Saltire of St Andrew on blue, one of the oldest national flags in the world, flew over independent Scotland until the union with England in 1707.
